WRITING BRIEF FOR A LINKED MATHEMATICAL OR TECHNICAL REFERENCE

Write a reference in the style of the Stacks Project and Jon Sterling's
Forester notes: a connected collection of precisely stated, individually
addressable definitions and results, with a readable exposition assembled
from them.

1. Structure concepts explicitly.

Every reusable definition should appear in a labelled Definition with its
own permanent tag. Separate definitions, lemmas, theorems, proofs, examples,
and open questions; avoid burying them in long paragraphs. For technical
documentation, use corresponding types such as specifications, procedures,
and examples where appropriate.

2. Help readers navigate.

Link definitions when concepts first appear, in statements of results, and
again where an argument relies on them. References should be frequent enough
to orient the reader without cluttering every occurrence. Link to the
specific definition or result, rather than a broad overview. Maintain a
definition index and a suggested reading order.

3. Keep one canonical version.

Each definition or result lives in one note with a stable address. Longer
reading accounts include those notes by transclusion, with connecting prose
explaining the argument. Edit the canonical note rather than duplicating
its content. Preserve addresses when titles or organization change. Keep
superseded notes with an explanation and a link to their replacements.

4. State claims precisely.

Specify the relevant objects, ambient spaces, assumptions, hypotheses, and
quantifiers. In mathematics, include coefficient fields and degrees where
needed. Use natural generality for definitions without unnecessary
abstraction. Distinguish a definition from a theorem characterizing the
defined object. Define notation before relying on it and use it consistently.

5. Make proofs and justifications accessible.

Keep the complete proof or justification reachable from its statement.
Identify logical dependencies and explain essential steps. Distinguish
these dependencies from background reading and ordinary cross-references.
Avoid circular arguments. For external inputs, cite the exact theorem or
section and check its hypotheses; distinguish direct applications from
analogies. Supply a separate argument when the cited result does not apply
directly.

6. Maintain a proper bibliography.

Give each paper, report, or other cited source a tagged reference entry.
Collect entries in a references tree and cite them in the substantive text.
Record authors, title, publication details or version, source links, and
precise locators for the material used. Preserve supplied source documents
and distinguish their original content from later editorial changes.

7. Be explicit about what is established.

Separate proved results, conjectures, computational evidence, and unresolved
gaps. State the scope and limitations of each claim. Do not extend a local
or conditional result beyond its hypotheses. Explain exactly what a
computation or test establishes; successful rendering, symbolic checks, or
numerical experiments do not by themselves verify an entire argument.

8. Write clear, economical, explanatory prose.

Provide enough connective discussion to show why each step is needed,
alongside precise statements and proofs. Use short, coherent paragraphs and
descriptive titles. Avoid walls of text, unexplained notation, unnecessary
repetition, and inflated language. A reader should be able to follow either
the continuous exposition or the links between individual notes.

When revising, update the canonical notes, their references and dependencies,
and the relevant reading accounts and indexes. Check links and inspect the
rendered reading path. Treat editorial and rendering checks separately from
substantive review of the claims.

- Jon Sterling's course forest: https://cl-l313.jonmsterling.com/01FE/
